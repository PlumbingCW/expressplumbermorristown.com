Before we arrive, stop using water, keep people and pets away from any standing sewage, and close off the room so it does not spread.

A sewer line carries everything from the house to the public main, and it fails in a few predictable ways. Pipes crack, joints slip, roots work in, and sections sag until waste pools. Repair starts with finding the exact spot and ends with a method that matches the damage, whether that is a small dig, a trenchless repair, or a longer replacement.

Sewer backup in the house

A true sewer backup is different from a single clogged sink, because waste returns through the lowest drains in the house. That is a sanitation problem as well as a plumbing one, so treat it as urgent.

  • Stop running every fixture, including the washing machine and dishwasher.
  • Do not flush, and keep the toilet lids closed if sewage is bubbling.
  • Move belongings off the floor and keep children and pets out of the area.
  • Wear gloves and boots if you must enter, and avoid touching the water.
  • Take photos of the affected area before cleanup, since they help with an insurance claim.

Cracked pipe and root intrusion

Older sewer laterals were often laid in clay, cast-iron or fiber pipe, all of which can crack from ground movement or corrode over time. A hairline crack lets in groundwater and soil, which feed roots and drag silt into the line. Eventually the pipe narrows until it can no longer carry waste.

A camera inspection separates a clog from a damaged pipe and measures how far the damage extends. If only one joint is open or one section has collapsed, a spot repair can restore the line without replacing all of it. The first step is often a camera inspection of the line.

Trenchless repair and pipe lining

Trenchless methods repair the pipe from the inside. For lining, a resin-saturated sleeve is pulled through the old pipe and cured in place, forming a smooth new wall within the old one. Pipe bursting pulls a new pipe through the path of the old one as the old pipe breaks apart. Both methods need access points rather than a long open trench.

Trenchless work is not always possible. A pipe with a deep sag, a collapse, or a sharp offset may need excavation, and the plumber should show you the camera footage that explains why. The advantage is that a lawn, driveway or garden bed often stays largely intact, which matters on the small lots common in a town of about three square miles.

After the Ida rain and heavy storms

In the September 1 to 2, 2021 remnants of Hurricane Ida, the Morristown area logged roughly 5.5 to 6.5 inches of rain in 24 hours, according to NJDEP figures reported at the time. Rain on that scale saturates the soil and loads the sewer system, and any weak joint in a private lateral is more likely to show itself afterward.

If your line slowed or backed up around a storm like that, keep the date and the symptoms in mind, because a pattern of recurring backups after rain is a strong reason to schedule a camera inspection instead of another quick cleaning. A cable pass or a jetting visit can buy time, but it will not close a gap.

Sewer or septic, and who maintains what

Most Morristown homes are on public sewer, and parts of town are served by the Morris Township Water Pollution Control Utility. Some properties outside the center of the region may still have septic tanks, and the rules for those are set locally. If you are not sure what your property has, ask the town construction office before any dig begins, since the answer changes what repair is possible. Neighbors in Morris Township face the same question.

Public sewer ownership generally stops at a point on the line, and the section from the house to that point is usually the homeowner's. A plumber can show you where your responsibility ends once the line has been traced.
