Detecting Underground Water Leaks at Home

A buried leak rarely announces itself. Your water meter, your bill and your lawn usually tell the story first.

The simplest way to detect an underground water leak is the meter test. Turn off every tap, appliance and ice maker, then watch the leak indicator on your water meter for fifteen minutes. If it keeps moving, water is escaping somewhere between the meter and a fixture, and a buried service line is a prime suspect.

Other signs include a sudden jump in your bill, a soggy patch of lawn on dry days, a drop in pressure and the sound of water when everything is off. A licensed plumber can pinpoint the leak with listening equipment and a camera, so you dig only where needed.

The meter test, step by step

Do this when nobody is using water, and do it at a quiet time such as early morning so a neighbor's laundry does not confuse the dial.

  • Shut off all faucets, the dishwasher, washing machine and ice maker.
  • Find your water meter, usually in the basement or in a box near the street.
  • Read the dial or note the small leak indicator triangle or wheel.
  • Wait fifteen minutes without using any water, then read it again.
  • If the number changed or the indicator spun, you have a leak.
  • Close the main shutoff valve and recheck. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house, and if it keeps moving, the leak is before the valve.

Other signs of a buried leak

Look for a wet or unusually green strip of grass, a sinkhole or soft spot, pooled water on pavement when it has not rained, or a warm patch in the ground if the line is hot water. In winter, melted snow over a line is a clue.

Inside, watch for low pressure at every fixture, a damp spot in the basement wall where the line enters, or a hissing sound near the main. Rust-colored water can also point to trouble with the service line.

Is the leak in my line or the utility's

Generally, the utility is responsible up to the meter or curb stop, and the homeowner is responsible after it, but boundaries vary. A call to your water supplier clears up where the line changes hands.

If the leak is between the street and your house, you are usually looking at a service line repair or replacement. If the leak is between the meter and the house, it is on your side. A plumber will find out precisely, then explain options.

Take a photo of the meter and the shutoff before you call. It speeds up the conversation with whichever party owns the line.

Reading your bill and your meter over time

Compare this month's usage with the same month last year. A rise that does not match guests, a new pool fill or a change in routine deserves a look. Write down the meter reading every night for a few days at the same hour, and compare the overnight change.

A steady overnight rise with nobody home points to a continuous leak. A pattern that spikes only when the irrigation runs points to the sprinkler system, which is a different repair.

How professionals find hidden leaks

Technicians start with a pressure test of the line, then use acoustic listening devices that pick up the sound of escaping water through soil and pipe. Thermal imaging can show a warm line, and tracer gas is used on harder-to-locate leaks.

Once the leak is marked, only a small area needs excavation. That is the reason to book leak detection instead of digging by trial and error, since random trenches damage lawns and walks and may miss the leak entirely.

Why you should not ignore it

Even a slow leak wastes water and can wash out the soil under a driveway, patio or walkway. Water pooling near a foundation wall can also seep into the basement.

Fixing it early usually means a smaller repair. Waiting can turn a pinhole into a full break, and a sudden break may need emergency burst pipe repair on a Saturday night.

What to do the moment you find one

Close the main shutoff, note the meter reading, and keep people off any soft ground, since a saturated area can give way. Shut off any water heater fed by the line so the element or burner does not run dry.

Photograph the wet area and the meter, then call a plumber. If water is flooding a yard or street, notify the utility as well.

If you rent, tell the landlord the same day and keep a record of when you reported it.

When DIY detection stops being enough

The meter test and a walk around the yard are good first steps, but they only confirm a leak exists. They do not find the exact spot. Dowsing rods and similar tricks do not work.

If your meter keeps moving after you have ruled out toilets and outdoor taps, call a plumber. Toilet flappers leak silently into the bowl, so add a few drops of food coloring to the tank first. If color shows in the bowl without flushing, fix that before blaming the yard.
