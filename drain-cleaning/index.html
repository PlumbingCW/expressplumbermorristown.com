Most household clogs sit within a few feet of a fixture, in the trap, the branch line, or the spot where a branch joins the main drain. A plumber works out which by watching how the other fixtures behave, then picks a hand tool, a cable machine, or a different method if the pipe is damaged. When the blockage turns out to be deeper than the house, the job moves toward main line cleaning.

Overflowing or stopped drain and what to do first

Call right away if water is rising in a sink, a floor drain is filling, or a toilet bubbles when you run a faucet. Those signs mean the blockage is affecting more than one fixture, and standing water can reach finished floors quickly. These steps help until the plumber arrives.

  • Stop using every sink, shower and appliance that drains, including the dishwasher and washing machine.
  • Close the angle stop under an overflowing fixture, or the main shutoff if you cannot find it.
  • Put towels around the drain and move rugs and boxes off the floor.
  • Skip chemical openers, because they sit in the pipe and can burn skin and eyes during the repair.
  • Notice which fixtures are slow and which are fully stopped, since that pattern points to where the clog sits.

Why ordinary clogs form in Morristown houses

Hair, soap film, cooking grease and wipes cause most of what we clear. Older houses add friction, because cast-iron drains roughen inside over the decades and snag debris that would slide through smooth plastic. Plenty of older homes around The Green and along Morris Street and South Street may still have drains of that type, so a fixture that clogs twice in one season could be catching on its own pipe walls.

In that case, ask about a jetting pass instead of another quick snaking.

Parts of Morristown are served by the Morris Township Water Pollution Control Utility, and the rest of the town sits on its own system. If every drain in the house slows at once, the cause is probably your sewer lateral or the public main rather than a single fixture. Check your bill or ask the town which utility serves your street, so you know who to phone if the plumber finds the blockage on the public side.

Snaking and cabling a stubborn line

For a sink or tub, a hand auger usually does the job. For a longer branch or a laundry standpipe, a plumber feeds a cable from a drum machine, using a cutting head sized to the pipe. The cable turns as it advances, breaking through hair and soap plugs and pulling the remnants back out where possible.

Cabling has limits. A snake often punches a hole through a clog and leaves a coating of grease on the pipe walls, so the drain runs freely for a few weeks and then slows again. When that happens, a camera look through a sewer inspection shows whether you have a heavy buildup, a bellied section, or a joint that has shifted.

Kitchen sink clogs

Kitchen drains clog differently from bathroom ones. Grease cools and hardens inside the trap and the horizontal run behind the wall, and food scraps pack against it until water barely moves. Double-basin sinks can hide the problem for a while, because one side drains into the other.

We pull and clean the trap when it is the culprit, then cable the branch line behind it and run hot water to confirm a steady flow. If a disposal is attached, we check it too, and a jammed unit may need a disposal repair before the line can be cleared. A basket strainer and a habit of wiping pans before washing prevent most repeat calls.

When a drain line leaks

A leaking drain line is not the same as a clog, though the two sometimes arrive together. The telltale signs are a damp patch on a basement ceiling that grows after a shower or laundry load, a sour smell near a wall, or a stain that dries out when no one is using water.

Because a drain pipe only leaks while water runs through it, the problem can look intermittent, and leak detection helps trace the exact joint or crack before anyone opens a wall.
